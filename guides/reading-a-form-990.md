# Reading a Form 990

Form 990 is the annual information return that most tax-exempt organizations file with the IRS. It is one of the most useful public documents for understanding a nonprofit, and it is written for the public as well as for the government.

This is a short guide to finding a filing and knowing what to look for. It is not tax, legal, or financial advice.

## How to find a filing

A good official starting point is the [IRS Charities and Nonprofits](https://www.irs.gov/charities-and-nonprofits) page, which points to Tax Exempt Organization Search and related tools. The IRS also maintains [Form 990 resources and tools](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/form-990-resources-and-tools) and a plain overview in [About Form 990](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-990).

Many filings are also collected in nonprofit profiles on [GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/search), which is part of [Candid](https://candid.org/).

Not every organization files the same form:

- Form 990 is the standard return for many larger organizations
- Form 990-EZ is a shorter return used by some smaller organizations
- Form 990-PF is filed by private foundations
- Form 990-N (the e-Postcard) is a brief annual notice used by some very small organizations

The IRS requires most tax-exempt organizations to file an annual return or notice unless an exception applies. If you cannot find a recent filing, that is worth noticing, and it is fair to ask the organization why.

## What to look for

The details vary by form, but a Form 990 usually offers a picture of the organization's programs, finances, and leadership. When I read one, I try to answer a few basic questions:

- **What do they say they do?** Look for the mission and the description of programs. This should match the story they tell on their website and in appeals.
- **How does money come in and go out?** Review revenue sources and expenses, including program, fundraising, and administrative costs. The right mix depends on the kind of work they do.
- **Who is in charge?** Note the board and key staff, and whether leadership and compensation are disclosed clearly.
- **Has anything unusual shown up?** Large deficits, missing information, or frequent leadership changes are not automatically a reason to walk away, but they are a reason to read more carefully.

A single year can be misleading. If several years of filings are available, look at the trend rather than one snapshot.

## A few cautions

- The Form 990 is a starting point, not a complete audit of the organization's work or impact.
- Smaller organizations that file Form 990-N will have much less public detail. In those cases, the organization's own materials and a direct conversation matter more.
- If you plan to deduct a gift, the IRS explains the donor rules separately in [Publication 526, Charitable Contributions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526). The organization's filing and your own tax records are related, but they are not the same thing.
