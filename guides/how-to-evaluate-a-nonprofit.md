# How to evaluate a nonprofit

A practical set of notes for looking at an organization before giving. This is not legal, tax, or financial advice. It draws on public guidance from Candid (including GuideStar), Charity Navigator, the IRS, and the BBB Wise Giving Alliance.

The aim is simple: confirm that an organization is real and in good standing, understand what it actually does, look at how it uses money, and see whether it is open about its work.

## 1. Confirm it is a recognized nonprofit

Start with the public record.

The IRS requires most tax-exempt organizations to file an annual information return or notice, unless an exception applies. Those filings include Form 990, Form 990-EZ, Form 990-PF, and the shorter Form 990-N (e-Postcard). As the IRS explains, Form 990 is also a way organizations share information with the public about their programs. For background, see [About Form 990](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-990) and [Form 990 resources and tools](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/form-990-resources-and-tools).

A good place to begin a lookup is the [IRS Charities and Nonprofits](https://www.irs.gov/charities-and-nonprofits) page, which points to Tax Exempt Organization Search and related tools.

[GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/search), part of [Candid](https://candid.org/), is another reliable place to find nonprofit profiles and IRS filings. Candid collects and publishes data on who nonprofits are and where the money goes.

Things worth confirming:

- The organization's legal name and Employer Identification Number (EIN)
- That it is recognized as tax-exempt, if it claims to be
- That recent filings exist, or that there is a clear reason they do not

## 2. Understand the work, not just the mission statement

A mission statement is a start, not the whole picture. Look for a plain explanation of:

- The problem the organization is trying to address
- What it actually does, and who it serves
- How it tells whether the work is helping

Charity Navigator's Encompass Rating System looks at this kind of question in its Impact & Measurement beacon: how a charity measures success, and how well its programs perform relative to the cost of running them. Their [ratings methodology](https://www.charitynavigator.org/about-us/our-methodology/ratings/) and [methodology overview](https://www.charitynavigator.org/about-us/our-methodology/) are useful background. They also publish a [guide to finding and evaluating effective nonprofits](https://www.charitynavigator.org/about-us/news-thought-leadership/2024-finding-and-evaluating-effective-nonprofits/).

The BBB Wise Giving Alliance includes measuring effectiveness in its [20 Standards for Charity Accountability](https://give.org/bbb-standards-for-charity-accountability), including whether the board periodically assesses results.

## 3. Read the finances with some care

Form 990 and, when available, audited financial statements show how money comes in and how it is spent. For a shorter walkthrough of finding and reading a filing, see [Reading a Form 990](reading-a-form-990.md). Useful things to look at include program spending, fundraising costs, reserves, and executive compensation — not as isolated scores, but as a picture of how the organization operates.

A few cautions:

- A low overhead number is not automatically a sign of a strong organization, and a higher one is not automatically a warning. Some kinds of work cost more to administer than others.
- Compare an organization to others doing similar work, rather than to a single ideal ratio.
- The BBB Wise Giving Alliance standards include finance measures, such as spending at least 65% of total expenses on program activities, and keeping fundraising costs reasonable relative to contributions. Those are their standards, not a universal rule, but they are a useful reference. See the [Standards for Charity Accountability](https://give.org/bbb-standards-for-charity-accountability).

Charity Navigator's Accountability & Finance beacon looks at whether a charity is accountable, transparent, and financially efficient and sustainable. GuideStar profiles and the Form 990 are often the easiest places to find this information in one spot.

## 4. Look at leadership, governance, and transparency

Ask whether the organization is open about its leadership, its board, and any recent challenges.

The BBB standards put a lot of weight on governance and oversight: an active, independent board, basic conflict-of-interest safeguards, and public information such as an annual report. Charity Navigator also reviews leadership and planning, as well as culture and compensation, as part of its Encompass Rating System.

Independent reviews can help, but they are a starting point, not a substitute for reading the organization's own materials:

- [Charity Navigator](https://www.charitynavigator.org/)
- [GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/), part of [Candid](https://candid.org/)
- [BBB Wise Giving Alliance charity reviews](https://give.org/charity-reviews) and [tips for donors](https://give.org/tips-for-donors)
- [CharityWatch](https://www.charitywatch.org/)
- [GiveWell](https://www.givewell.org/), which publishes in-depth research on high-impact, cost-effective giving opportunities

## 5. Decide whether it is a good fit

After the basic checks, the remaining question is personal: does this organization do work I care about, and is supporting it a thoughtful way to help? A highly rated organization in a cause I do not care about is not automatically the right gift, and a smaller local group can still be a careful, meaningful choice if the basics check out.
