# Questions to ask before you donate

A short list of questions I find useful before sending a gift. They are meant to slow the decision down just enough to be thoughtful, not to turn giving into a research project. For a fuller walkthrough of due diligence, see [How to evaluate a nonprofit](how-to-evaluate-a-nonprofit.md).

## 1. Do I know exactly who I am giving to?

**Why it matters:** Many appeals use similar names, and some organizations sound official without being the group you have in mind. Confirming the legal name, website, and Employer Identification Number (EIN) helps make sure the gift reaches the right place.

## 2. Is this organization recognized as a nonprofit, and can I check that myself?

**Why it matters:** If an organization claims tax-exempt status, that should be visible in the public record. The [IRS Charities and Nonprofits](https://www.irs.gov/charities-and-nonprofits) page and a recent Form 990 (see [About Form 990](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-990)) are good places to start, as are profiles on [GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/search).

## 3. What problem are they trying to solve, and how clearly do they explain their approach?

**Why it matters:** A moving story is not the same as a clear plan. If it is hard to tell what the organization actually does, it will also be hard to tell whether a gift is likely to help.

## 4. How do they know the work is helping?

**Why it matters:** Good intentions are not the same as results. Look for a straightforward account of outcomes, even if the measures are imperfect. Charity Navigator's Impact & Measurement work is one independent way to look at this; see their [ratings methodology](https://www.charitynavigator.org/about-us/our-methodology/ratings/).

## 5. How do they use the money they raise?

**Why it matters:** Program spending, fundraising costs, reserves, and leadership pay all help describe how an organization operates. No single ratio tells the whole story, but the numbers should make sense for the kind of work they do. Form 990 filings and evaluator reports are useful here.

## 6. Are they open about leadership, governance, and hard news?

**Why it matters:** Organizations that publish board information, financials, and honest updates are easier to trust. The [BBB Standards for Charity Accountability](https://give.org/bbb-standards-for-charity-accountability) put a lot of weight on an active, independent board and on making basic information public.

## 7. Has an independent evaluator looked at them, and what did they find?

**Why it matters:** A review is not a substitute for your own judgment, but it can surface issues you might miss. [Charity Navigator](https://www.charitynavigator.org/), [GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/), the [BBB Wise Giving Alliance](https://give.org/charity-reviews), [CharityWatch](https://www.charitywatch.org/), and [GiveWell](https://www.givewell.org/) are a few places to look, depending on the organization.

## 8. Is this appeal timely, or am I being rushed?

**Why it matters:** Urgent asks are sometimes justified, especially after a disaster. They can also leave too little time to check the basics. The BBB Wise Giving Alliance collects [tips for donors](https://give.org/tips-for-donors), including notes on disaster relief and other high-pressure appeals. A short pause is often enough.

## 9. Does this cause, and this organization, actually fit what I care about?

**Why it matters:** A well-run organization in a cause I do not care about is not automatically the right gift. After the basic checks, it is fair to choose the work that matters most to me.

## 10. Is a gift of money the most useful thing I can offer right now?

**Why it matters:** Sometimes money is exactly what is needed. Sometimes an organization is asking for something more specific, or a local group may need time, goods, or a different kind of help. It is worth reading the ask closely rather than assuming.

## 11. If I give, can I do it directly and keep a record?

**Why it matters:** Giving through the organization's official website or a known, reputable channel reduces the chance of a gift going astray. A receipt also helps if the gift is tax-deductible; the IRS has guidance on this in [Publication 526, Charitable Contributions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526).

## 12. Will I be comfortable checking back later?

**Why it matters:** Thoughtful giving does not end when the gift is sent. If I would not be comfortable looking the organization up again in a year — to see what they did, and whether I would give again — that is worth noticing now.
