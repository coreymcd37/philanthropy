# Spotting charity scams

A practical set of notes on common warning signs and how to check an appeal before giving. This is not legal advice. It draws on public guidance from the BBB Wise Giving Alliance, the IRS, the National Association of State Charity Officials (NASCO), and official U.S. government pages on scams and fraud, including the FTC's fraud reporting site.

Most fundraising appeals are made in good faith. The goal here is not to become suspicious of every request, but to slow down when something feels off and to know where to check.

## Common warning signs

A single one of these does not prove an appeal is a scam, but several together are a reason to pause:

- **Pressure to give immediately.** Urgent asks are sometimes justified, especially after a disaster. They can also leave too little time to check the basics. The BBB Wise Giving Alliance collects [tips for donors](https://give.org/tips-for-donors), including notes on disaster relief and other high-pressure appeals.
- **An unsolicited call, text, email, or direct message.** Be careful with unexpected requests, especially if the person is reluctant to share the organization's legal name, mailing address, or Employer Identification Number (EIN), or pushes you toward an unusual payment method.
- **A name that sounds official, or very close to a well-known charity.** Similar names are a common source of confusion. Confirm that you are looking at the organization you have in mind, not one with a nearby name.
- **A request for cash, gift cards, a wire transfer, or payment to an individual.** Legitimate organizations usually have a straightforward way to give through their official channels and can provide a record of the gift.
- **Vague answers about the work or the money.** If it is hard to tell what the organization does, who it helps, or how a gift will be used, that is worth noticing.

USA.gov notes that imposters can pretend to be a charity (or a government agency) in order to win your trust and take money or personal information. See [Imposter scams](https://www.usa.gov/imposter-scams) and [Scams and fraud](https://www.usa.gov/scams-and-fraud).

## How to verify an appeal

A few checks are usually enough:

1. **Confirm the exact organization.** Write down the legal name, website, and EIN if you have them. Do not rely only on a name you heard over the phone or a link in an unsolicited message.
2. **Check the public record.** The IRS [Tax Exempt Organization Search](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/search-for-tax-exempt-organizations) tool lets you look up an organization's tax-exempt status and filings, including Form 990 returns, the list of organizations eligible to receive tax-deductible contributions (Publication 78 data), and the automatic revocation list. An organization's tax-exempt status is automatically revoked if it does not file required annual returns or notices for three consecutive years, though some organizations on that list may later be reinstated. The [IRS Charities and Nonprofits](https://www.irs.gov/charities-and-nonprofits) page is a good official starting point. For help reading a filing, see [Reading a Form 990](reading-a-form-990.md).
3. **Look for an independent review.** [Charity Navigator](https://www.charitynavigator.org/), [GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/) (part of [Candid](https://candid.org/)), and [BBB Wise Giving Alliance charity reviews](https://give.org/charity-reviews) can add useful context. A missing review is not automatically a warning, especially for a small or new group, but a review that raises concerns is worth reading carefully.
4. **Give through official channels.** If you decide to give, use the organization's official website or a known, reputable channel, and keep a receipt. For a longer list of questions, see [Questions to ask before you donate](questions-to-ask-before-you-donate.md).

## If something feels wrong

It is fine to wait. A legitimate organization can usually give you time to check.

- Report suspected fraud to the Federal Trade Commission at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).
- USA.gov also explains [where to report a scam](https://www.usa.gov/where-report-scams) if you are not sure which agency to contact.
- Many states register charities and accept complaints. NASCO keeps a directory of [state charity regulators and consumer protection agencies](https://www.nasconet.org/resources/state-government), which is a reliable way to find the right office.
